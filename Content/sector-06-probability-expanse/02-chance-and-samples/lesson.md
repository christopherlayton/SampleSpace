# Chance and Samples

Statistics exists because we almost never measure everyone.

| Word | Means | Written |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | everyone you want to draw conclusions about | |
| **Sample** | the people you actually measured | |
| **Parameter** | a number describing the population (fixed, usually unknown) | Greek: μ, σ, ρ |
| **Statistic** | a number describing the sample (known, varies from sample to sample) | Roman: x̄ or *M*, *s* or *SD*, *r* |

Inferential statistics uses statistics to estimate parameters, **and says how wrong the estimate might be**. That second part depends on how the sample was drawn.

## Probability

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 giving the long-run proportion of times something happens. A fair die lands on 6 with probability 1/6: over thousands of rolls, the proportion of sixes settles near 0.167, though any short run can wander.

## Random sampling

In a **simple random sample**, every member of the population has the same chance of being chosen. Random samples can still be unlucky, but *how* unlucky they tend to be can be calculated. That calculation, the standard error, is what the rest of this sector builds.

> **Your dissertation:** most education and psychology studies use convenience samples (volunteers, one school, one course). The statistics still work as if the sample were random, so the honest thing is to say who your sample represents and to describe it carefully in your Method section.
