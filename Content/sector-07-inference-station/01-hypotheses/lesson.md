# Hypotheses

Welcome to Inference Station. Every test you'll run in your dissertation starts the same way: by writing down two hypotheses about the **population**.

| | Says | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **H₀**, the null | nothing is going on: no difference, no relationship | μ(mindfulness) = μ(control) |
| **H₁**, the alternative | something is | μ(mindfulness) ≠ μ(control) |

The test assumes H₀ is true and asks whether the data look surprising under that assumption. Surprising enough (p < α) and you **reject H₀**. Otherwise you **fail to reject** it; you never "accept" or "prove" it.

## One-sided or two-sided

A **two-sided** H₁ (≠) counts a difference in either direction. A **one-sided** H₁ (< or >) only counts one direction and puts all of α in that tail. Use one-sided only when theory fixes the direction *before* you see the data, and say so in your proposal.

## Writing hypotheses for your dissertation

Committees expect them in symbols and in words:

> H₀: There is no difference in mean post-test anxiety between students who complete the mindfulness programme and those who don't (μ₁ = μ₂).
> H₁: Mean post-test anxiety differs between the two groups (μ₁ ≠ μ₂).

## A near-complete population

The Crew Log isn't a sample of astronauts; it's almost all of them. So "is there a difference in the population?" has a trivial answer: look at the log. A test asks something subtler: **is the pattern bigger than chance variation in the process that produced it would typically be?** That's the question this sector's review answers for the share of women among first-time flyers.
