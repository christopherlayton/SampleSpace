# Reading p

You've seen where p comes from. Now the harder part: reading it correctly, because published papers often don't.

## What p is

**p = the probability of results at least as extreme as yours, if H₀ were true.**

Small p: the data would be surprising in a world where H₀ holds, so you have evidence against H₀. That's all.

## What p isn't

| Common reading | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| "the probability H₀ is true" | p *assumes* H₀ is true |
| "the probability the result is due to chance" | same mistake in other words |
| "a big effect" | p shrinks with sample size as well as with effect size |
| "p > .05, so there's no effect" | a small sample can easily miss a real effect |

## Report more than p

The fix is to report three things together: the **test** (with its statistic and df), the **effect size**, and a **confidence interval**. A 95% CI and a two-sided test at α = .05 always agree: p < .05 exactly when the interval excludes the null value (usually 0). But the CI also shows how big the effect might plausibly be.

> **APA note (7th ed.):** italic *p*, no leading zero, exact to two or three decimals: *p* = .032. Below .001 write *p* < .001. Don't use "approaching significance" or asterisks in text. Report effect sizes and CIs alongside the test, e.g. *t*(36) = 2.21, *p* = .033, *d* = 0.72 for two groups of 19.
